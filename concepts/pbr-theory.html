PBR theory — in three pictures

Companion to GamePBR step 10. The math behind physically-based shading, made draggable. Everything here is the groundwork for the Cook-Torrance BRDF in step 11.

1 · The rendering equation

The light leaving a surface toward your eye is the sum, over every incoming direction on the hemisphere, of arriving light times how much the surface bounces it your way — times a cosine that accounts for how slanted light spreads thin.

Lo(wo) = ∫Ω  f(wo, wi) · Li(wi) · (N·wi)  dwi
N·wi (cosine term) = 0.82
Drag on the canvas to orbit. Lower the light and the cosine — and the shading — fades to zero at the horizon.

2 · What a BRDF is — and energy conservation

A BRDF f(wo, wi) is the shape of the bounce. Below: the diffuse (Lambert) lobe is a fat cosine hemisphere; the specular lobe is a thin petal around the mirror direction. The number that matters is the hemispherical reflectance — integrate the lobe and you learn what fraction of incident light leaves. Physical means it never exceeds 1.0.

raw specular reflectance R = 2.61 (red > 1 = reflects more than it receives)

This is the un-normalized step-09 lobe, the one the renderer actually used. For a broad highlight it reflects several times the incident energy; crank the exponent and it loses almost all of it. That untethering is exactly what step 11's GGX normalization fixes.

3 · The faults this names

Hold the baseline up to the constraints: no 1/π on the diffuse, an arbitrary specular tint, no Fresnel, no geometric shadowing, and a specular lobe that fails the energy bound above. Cook-Torrance (step 11) replaces the two lobes with terms — D, G, F — that each have a physical job and together stay under 1.0.
